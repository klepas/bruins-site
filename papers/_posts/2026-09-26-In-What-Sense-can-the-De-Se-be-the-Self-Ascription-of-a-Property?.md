abstract: |
  This paper challenges the view that representation and illocution *de se* can be understood as the self-ascription of a property (or, for current purposes, equivalently, a propositional function). The view is very popular, being endorsed by numerous otherwise quite different philosophers, e.g., Chisholm, Loar, Lewis, Feit and Speaks, while others have endorsed it partially, e.g., the later Perry, Recanati and Musholt. It is argued, however, that the basic idea, as articulated in Loar 1976, is incoherent: it cannot accommodate the possibility of a subject’s merely ‘entertaining’ a content *de se* without having it in a representational or illocutionary mode. Any content must be in this sense ‘entertainable’ since otherwise it would be impossible for a subject to know it for the content it is without representing or illocuting it. This would in turn make it impossible simply to understand a content as a *putative* reason for belief or action without automatically accepting it as an *actual* reason. Thereby self-conscious deliberation about whether to accept or reject content would be rendered impossible. Any sense in which a representation or illocution can be regarded as the self-ascription of a property (or propositional function) presupposes a predicative sense of self-ascription at the level of content, which entails that in such content *de se* there is element corresponding to whatever pronoun is required for perspicuous characterisation of content—either the first-person pronoun or the asterisked third-person.

# In What Sense (or Senses!) can the *De Se* be the Self-Ascription of a Property or Propositional Function?

## Introduction—Castañeda’s (S)-Used Third-Person Pronoun

Consider two literal and true assertions, one of the sentence “Mary believes that she\* is happy,” typically by someone other than Mary, the other of the sentence “I believe that I am happy” by Mary herself. Both report one and the same representational reality, belief, namely, on Mary’s part that she\* is happy. The first sentence is an example taken from Hector-Neri Castañeda[^1] and the asterisk contained in it plays the role Castañeda gave it: the asterisk makes explicit that the use made of the third-person pronoun is what he called an (S)-use.[^2] When a third-person pronoun is (S)-used, the user of it refers to a subject of representation or locution in this latter’s capacity as subject—subject of the representation or locution whose content the pronoun is being used to characterise perspicuously, such that it contributes to capturing the causal role imparted by content. So as (S)-used, the pronoun occurs in a clause subordinate to an appropriate intensional context, in Castañeda’s example, the intensional context ‘Mary believes …’. As for the clause itself, this will typically[^3] be a ‘that’-clause, in the example, ‘that she\* is happy’. Evidently, reference accomplished by an (S)-used pronoun is anaphoric: the pronoun inherits the referent, but not the reference, of the singular term in the intensional context to which the clause containing it is subordinate. Since both sentences, as literally and truly asserted by their respective speakers, depict one and the same representational reality, the ‘that’-clauses ‘that she\* is happy’ and ‘that I am happy’ characterise one and the same content _qua_ what is believed. In the literature, this one content is called _de se_, i.e., about him-, her- or itself since the Latin pronoun ‘_se_’ is gender-neutral. And it renders any representation or locution of which it is content itself de se. What, however, _is_ content _de se_?

## Section I: What is the *De Se*?—Three Kinds of Answer

Those influenced to one degree or another by Frege will construe the content expressed by both clauses in strict analogy to that expressed by the clause ‘that Mary is happy’. They will insist that just as the singular term ‘Mary’ expresses a sense in virtue of which it refers to Mary, so, too, the asterisked third-person pronoun expresses a sense in virtue of which it refers, on the occasion, to Mary. Since this sense, this mode of givenness, must be the same as that expressed by the first-person pronoun in the first-personal counterpart of the clause, Frege himself construed this _element in content_ as a distinctive mode in which Mary is given to herself, namely, as herself. Thereby he construes this element in the content of belief as _itself_ first-personally indexical; it is the “specific and original way” a self-conscious subject such as Mary “is given to himself,” given in a way “he is to no other.” (Frege 1918/1976, p. 39; my translation) Similarly, for Peacocke, Musholt and Chalmers, it is a self- or first-person concept; for the later Perry, a self-notion; for Castañeda himself, an ‘I’-guise; for Evans an ‘I’-idea; and for Soames a thinking of something “in the first-person way”.[^4]

In conclusion, I will briefly argue that the whole idea of *first-personally indexical* elements in content is muddled. Yet this idea does better than any Russellian construal since construal of content in general as an _n_-tuple of referents and properties or relations must fail to distinguish between contents _de se_ and contents not _de se_. We have an impasse and what I shall call _the property-theoretic or propositionally functional conception of content **de se**_ has emerged in part as a way out of it, that is, as a way of avoiding both first-personally indexical senses and bare Russellian referents. The conception is very popular: many have embraced it outright, for example, Roderick Chisholm, Brian Loar, David Lewis, Neil Feit and Jeff Speaks, while others have endorsed it partially. Thus, the later Perry, François Recanati and Kristina Musholt conceive some forms of representation *de se* in terms of it, those forms, namely, which cannot plausibly be regarded as cases of actual capacity for “genuine self-conscious thought,” for what Musholt calls “explicit self-representation” (Musholt 2015, p. 26), which entails explicitly first-personal awareness. Such awareness is surely implicated neither in the representations of many animals nor in ground-floor perceptual experience, as when a jaywalker sees a large truck hurtling towards him\* or her\*. So in these cases there is no _first-personally indexical_ element in content and from this one concludes that in them no element at all corresponds to the asterisked third-person pronoun.

It will not be possible here to examine all variants of the conception. Fortunately, all variants are governed by a master idea, one most succinctly and generally presented by Brian Loar in his paper “The Semantics of Singular Terms,” so I will focus upon this. Specifically, I will examine what Loar calls “the self-ascriptive belief relation” (Loar 1976, p. 358) in order to determine whether the property-theoretic or propositionally functional conception can deal adequately, indeed at all, with an issue implicit in recent discussion of how the conception might accommodate the _communication_ of contents _de se_. As we shall see, the issue runs deep. It begins as the question of whether a property-theoretic or propositionally functional conception of representation and content _de se_ can accommodate the possibility of a subject’s merely entertaining, as one says, such content, that is, knowing it for what it is without having it as a content in any representational mode—this because one is deliberating about the correctness of having it in this or that representational mode.[^5] The murky phrase ‘entertaining a content’ gestures, however, towards the deeper question of whether the conception can accommodate the possibility of communication between _rational_, hence _self-conscious_[^6] subjects. Such communication, since it involves the giving and taking of reasons, also involves, at least potentially if not always actually, a hearer _first_ recognising something as given to it\* to be taken as a reason, _then_ either taking or rejecting it as such a reason and responding appropriately. So in such communication there is, at least potentially if not always actually, two distinct aspects: first, _understanding_ of what is said as a putative reason for belief or action, then _acceptance_ of it as an actual reason. Can the property-theoretic or propositionally functional conception accommodate this?

Yet neither understanding nor acceptance of content is restricted to communication. Rational, hence self-conscious subjects are as such capable of wondering whether there is sufficient reason to believe contents or to make them true. Consequently, such a subject must be able to wonder whether it\* is F, whether something F standing in relation R<sub>n</sub> to it\* is G or whether it\* should perform the action A, and the like. The deepest question is thus whether the property-theoretic or propositionally functional conception can accommodate the capacity, first, for non-committal understanding of something as a potential reason, then for the acceptance of it as an actual one. In other words, the deepest question is whether the conception is compatible with the very possibility of rational, hence self-conscious subjectivity. Just this shows something dear at least to my heart: addressing the question of the _de se_ is an exercise in transcendental philosophy.

## Section II: Loar’s Self-Ascriptive Belief Relation

David Lewis cites Loar’s suggestion that one analyse “certain exceptional beliefs as self-ascription of properties (or “propositional functions”).” (Lewis 1979, footnote 4, p. 519) These exceptional beliefs are precisely beliefs de se, i.e., ones with contents de se, as is shown by Loar’s example of Watson, who, having seen “himself through some sort of mirror device” and concluded that “he is seeing a burglar,” “… believes of Watson that he is a burglar, but he does not believe himself[\*] to be a burglar.” (Loar 1976, p. 358) Loar now goes on, first to label such beliefs self-ascriptive, then to suggest understanding the label as follows:

>If Cynthia believes that she[\*] has the flu, then what is true is: B\*(Cynthia, _y has the flu_). (Loar 1976, p. 358)[^7]

In effect, Loar is suggesting an interpretation of sentences such as “Cynthia believes that she\* has the flu” and, of course, of their first-personal counterparts according to which the representation they attribute is a relation of their subject not to a proposition but to a propositional function or, equivalently, at least for current purposes, to a property. Cynthia stands, not in a relation of belief to a proposition,[^8] but in a relation of belief\*, i.e., B\*(_x_, _y is F_), to the propositional function or property ‘_y has the flu_’. As Loar puts it, Cynthia “… is related by the self-ascriptive belief relation to that propositional function.” (Loar 1976, p. 358)

Evidently, Loar introduces “the self-ascriptive belief relation” by appeal to a sentence of the form “S believes that it\* is F.” He starts with something we ordinarily understand, namely, Cynthia’s believing that she\* has the flu and the kind of sentence used to characterise it, in order to introduce something we do not ordinarily understand, viz., the two-place relation B\*(_x_, _y has the flu_). At this point, it might seem that Loar is stipulatively defining the relation B\*(_x_, _y  is F_) to take subjects of belief S and properties F or propositional functions ‘_y is F_’ as its arguments and to hold just in case S believes that he\*, she\* or it\* is F. This in turn might lead one to ask what good his suggestion could serve. Surely its _prima facie_ stipulative nature entails that it presupposes, hence cannot elucidate, the notion of belief _de se_?[^9]

Loar and those who follow him would presumably respond as follows: his suggestion is to be understood as the postulation of a hypothesis as to how belief _de se_ not only can but must be understood, given the success of a larger theoretical agenda. That belief _de se_ is not merely stipulated to be, but genuinely explicated by, the so-called self-ascriptive belief relation is shown by the success of the agenda. So what is the agenda? For Loar it is to provide what he regards as a Fregean answer to the question of how singular terms refer. In particular, he seeks to show that “the function of a singular term is to introduce” what he calls “an individual concept into what is meant or expressed on its particular uses.” (Loar 1976, p. 354) “(E)very normal utterance of a singular term” stands in “appropriate relation” to an individual concept such that “an object is referred to only if it satisfies the individual concept.” (Loar 1976, p. 355)

An individual concept thus appears to be some individuating characterisation of the referent of a singular term expressed by the latter on the occasion of its utterance, that is, use. This is not to claim that the characterisation constitutes the _meaning_ of the singular term, for according to Loar this need not be so. Consider the singular term ‘the daughter of the reporter’ in the sentence “‘The daughter of the reporter is a geologist’” (Loar 1976, p. 365). The individual concept expressed on the occasion of its use by this complex singular term may in fact be perspicuously articulated by another more complex and crucially also relational singular term, e.g., ‘the daughter of the reporter whom we see there’—see Loar 1976, p. 365. Singular terms do not map mechanically on to the individual concepts which pick out their referents, thereby enabling them to refer. What individual concept is expressed is a contextual, hence occasional matter. Crucially, it is also a relational matter, in particular, a relation of the utterance, hence the utterer, to the referent, even when, as in the example, the relation is quite complex. For in the example the relation is mediated by a relation of the referent to some other item which itself stands in such and such relation to the utterance and its utterer, namely, the reporter.

This relation of referent to utterance and utterer creates, however, a problem. Reference is something under the rational control of the referrer. The latter thus typically knows, or at least claims to know, what it\* is referring to. In order to secure the possibility of this, the relational component of an individual concept introduces what the referrer itself would typically express by means of the first-person pronoun, another by means of the asterisked third-person pronoun. In Loar’s example, the referent of the singular term ‘the daughter of the reporter’, on the hypothetically envisaged occasion of its use, is also the daughter of someone “we see there,” as the referrer S and this latter’s conversation partner S<sup>&#9670;</sup> would put it. As another might put it, this referent is the daughter of the person the referrer S sees there\*, possibly in front of it\*, whom S also assumes S<sup>&#9670;</sup> similarly to see there\*, presumably in front of S, which total situation is mutually known to S and S<sup>&#9670;</sup>. Loar gestures towards this when he writes,

> The objects of my beliefs and utterances are often individuated for me by virtue of relations in which they uniquely stand to me. So, in a sense, beliefs about them are beliefs about myself … . (Loar 1976, p. 357)
He then concludes that “understanding the nature of the individuating conceptions I have of other objects requires some understanding of the nature of my beliefs about myself. (Loar 1976, p. 357)

With this, he arrives at the problem:

>Having a belief about oneself clearly does not require having a purely qualitative individuating conception of oneself: one need only imagine the early stages of amnesia to see that. Nor is my believing myself to be such and such simply a matter of having a de re belief about myself. (Loar 1976, pp. 357-358)

In other words, in order to ensure the character of reference as under the rational control of the referrer, at least some individuating conceptions must be such that their perspicuous linguistic expression involves a reference to the utterer, one, however, _not_ enabled by such an individuating conception, since otherwise a vicious regress would ensue.

At this point it becomes clear why Loar suggests that one understand belief _de se_ as “the self-ascriptive belief[\*] relation.” In referring to the daughter of the reporter, S is perceptually related to a person standing in front of S whom S sees there and whom S knows to be both the reporter. So at time _t_ and location _l_ S perceives the reporter standing then\* and (over) there\*,[^10] hence presumably believes the reporter to be standing then\* and there\*, in front of it\*. Consequently, if we allow that S’s perceptual experience is also self-ascriptive,[^11] what is true according to Loar is both that P\*(S, _t_, _l_, _The reporter is standing at y and z in front of x_); and that B\*(S, _t_, _l_, _The reporter is standing at y and z in front of x_)—_y_, _z_ and _x_ being, of course, variables ranging over times, locations and subjects while the expression ‘_The reporter is standing at y and z in front of x_’ connotes a propositional function.

Note now that in these self-ascriptive reformulations, reference to S is accomplished in a fashion entirely concordant with Loar’s more or less Fregean conception of it, for it is accomplished by whatever individual concept is expressed on the occasion by the singular term ‘S’. Insofar as the term is understood as a name, the individual concept will be that of being the individual or person called ‘S’ since in the case of a name N “(t)he individual concept … essential to what is meant … has the form _the F called N_.” (Loar 1976, p. 370) So when S’s representations are understood in Loar’s self-ascriptive fashion, they show themselves to be contentful in such a fashion that their perspicuous linguistic expression unproblematically involves a Fregean reference to S. One need not conclude that not all reference is enabled by an individual concept expressed by the use of a term, which conclusion is the threat _prima facie_ presented to a Fregean conception by both the first-person and the asterisked third-person pronoun.[^12]

Thus, if Loar’s account of how singular terms in general refer is true, as would be evidenced by successful contribution to “a semantical theory for a language” (Loar 1976, p. 353), then so, too, is his account of belief _de se_ in terms of “the self-ascriptive belief relation” B\*(_x_, _y is F_). But what exactly is this relation? And how does the idea generalise, as it surely must. There is, after all, not only belief _de se_ but as just seen, presumably also perception _de se_. Indeed, there is also desire _de se_, as when Fido the small pug desires that it\* not be attacked by the large pit bull charging aggressively towards it\*: D\*(Fido, _y is not attacked by the large pit bull charging aggressively towards y_). These questions become pressing once one realises that the general self-ascriptive idea, hence the property-theoretic or propositionally functional conception of content _de se_, cannot be justified _solely_ by appeal to its capacity to avert a threat to an otherwise successful, more or less Fregean semantics of singular terms. This is no more true of the more or less Fregean semantic theory envisaged by Loar than it is of a principal competitor to such a theory, namely, a possible world semantics. When, for example, it is suggested that if Cynthia has the flu, what is true is that the set of possible worlds in which Cynthia has the flu contains the actual world, one may insist, indeed many have insisted, that more be said about what a possible world is. One does not rest content, and many have not rested content, either with whatever initial spelling out is provided by the adjective ‘possible’ and the noun ‘world’ or with the powerful formal machinery one can develop out of the suggestion by appeal to a well-understood set theory. One acknowledges that the notion of a possible world requires further elaboration since _prima facie_ it involves all sorts of problematic commitments.

Something similar applies in the case of the self-ascriptive belief relation. True, one has the symbolism ‘B\*(Cynthia, _y has the flu_)’, a pre-theoretical understanding of properties and functions as applying to individuals, and the insight that if the symbolism is understood as a sentence necessarily equivalent in what it expresses to what is expressed by the sentence “Cynthia believes that she\* has the flu,” key goals of “a [more or less Fregean] semantical theory for a language” (Loar 1976, p. 353) can be realised. But more must be said about what “the self-ascriptive belief relation” is since however neatly it might solve a problem for the envisaged “semantical theory,” there might be some difficulty lurking within it, just as within the notion of a possible world there is the problem of how strongly we are to understand claims that _there are_ such things. No doubt whatever problem there might be for “the self-ascriptive belief relation” is more subtle, consisting presumably in failure to accommodate something one expects representation and content _de se_ to be able to do. Might there be something one expects them to enable which, when they are conceived self-ascriptively, they cannot? Only by saying more can one answer this question.

Now at least part of what more is required consists in providing some account of how the self-ascriptive relation B\*(_x_, _y is F_), when supplied with appropriate arguments, might constitute a cognitive representation perspicuously characterisable in those ordinary everyday terms which employ the asterisked third-person pronoun. _How_ might B\*(Cynthia, _y has the flu_) make it the case that the sentence “Cynthia believes that she\* has the flu” expresses a distinctive truth—one such that perspicuous characterisation requires the kind of reference accomplished by the asterisked third-person or, as the case may be, the first-person pronoun? There is surely only one way. One must say, not just of Cynthia’s belief, but also of the small pug’s seeing a large pit bull charging aggressively towards it\* and its desire that it\* not be attacked by the large pit bull charging aggressively towards it\*, that “the essential self-relatedness [expressed by either the asterisked third-person or the first-person pronoun] is provided by a fact that is _external_ to the content of experience itself, namely, by the mode of presentation.” (Musholt 2015, p. 80) In other words, one must allow that sometimes _representational mode_, not “a self-referring component” (Musholt 2015, p. 80) within representation itself, can determine that ordinary, everyday perspicuous characterisation of content by means of a ‘that’-clause requires use of either the first-person or the asterisked third-person pronoun. Perhaps the defining feature and accomplishment of a distinctively _self-ascriptive_ representational mode R\*(_x_, _y is F_) is that _it_ renders the subject _qua_ subject constitutive of the identity of content _qua_ what is individuated by the appropriate ‘that’-clause. If so, we can explain why, in the case of such representational modes, use of either the first-person or the asterisked third-person pronoun is required for ordinary, everyday perspicuous characterisation by means of a ‘that’-clause.

## Section III: The Transcendental Analytic of the _De Se_

Yet there is a problem. In his paper “_De Se_ Communication—Centered or Uncentered?,”[^13] Peter Pagin argues that if one attempts in this way to account for the fact, indeed, the necessity, of either the first-person or the asterisked third-person pronoun in ordinary, everyday perspicuous characterisation by means of a ‘that’-clause, one encounters a serious problem which “comes to light when considering communication” although it does not concern, as Pagin doubtless realises, communication alone. He first characterises the alleged accomplishment of the cognitively self-ascriptive representational mode B\*(_x_, _y is F_): it ensures that the subject of an instance of the mode, i.e., the value of ‘_x_’, “is not represented … . Rather, it is the _belief-mode_ [more accurately put, the _B\*-mode_], or _belief- force_ [once again, more accurately put, the _B\*-force_], in the form of self-ascription, that makes the connection” (Pagin 2016, p. 275) between the subject _qua_ subject and the subject _qua_ what is referred to in the ‘that’-clause one would ordinarily use in order to characterise the representation perspicuously—a ‘that’-clause containing the first-person or asterisked third-person pronoun, of course. He then indicates the problem: this conception of how the subject _qua_ subject becomes constitutive of the identity of content “means that a subject cannot merely _entertain_ the … thought,” that is, the content, “that she[\*] has a certain property; what she entertains is just the content of the representation of the _property_.” According to Pagin,

>(t)his does not matter for _belief_ [i.e., for B\*], which by definition has force. But it does matter for communication, because the hearer does not automatically _believe_ the speaker: typically we first _understand_, then perhaps form a new belief. (Pagin 2016, p. 275)

The problem lying at the heart of any property-theoretic or propositionally functional conception is therefore this: how can a subject S grasp something as what one would ordinarily describe as the content that it\*, S, is F, i.e., without instantiating the self-ascriptive belief relation B\*(_x_, _y is F_)? Most generally put, how can a subject S understand something as what one would ordinarily describe as the content that it\*, S, is F without instantiating some self-ascriptive relation or other—say, the self-ascriptive perception relation P\*(_x_, _y is F_), the self-ascriptive desire relation D\*(_x_, _y is F_), or whatever other such relations there might be? Any version of the property-theoretic or propositionally functional conception, however it is conceived more specifically, must assume that the self-ascriptive representational or illocutionary relation of a subject to a property or propositional function constitutes the whole representation or illocution as the representation or illocution it is, possessed of a certain representational or illocutionary force. Yet it must also explain—for how else is one to explain it?—the character of such representation or illocution _de se_ as requiring for its ordinary, everyday perspicuous characterisation by means of a ‘that’-clause use of either the first-person or the asterisked third-person pronoun. One and the same thing, the self-ascriptive representational or illocutionary relation, accounts for both.

It now follows that a subject S cannot entertain what one would ordinarily describe as the content that it\*, S, is F without standing in some self-ascriptive representational or illocutionary relation. As applied specifically to Loar’s self-ascriptive belief relation, S cannot entertain the content that it\* is F, i.e., wonder whether it\* is F, without believing that it\* is F. And in general, by making any self-ascriptive representational or illocutionary relation responsible both for representation or illocution _de se_ as a whole; and for character as having (what one would ordinarily, pre-theoretically describe as) a content _de se_: one collapses the grasping or understanding of content into the acceptance of it—in the case of the self-ascriptive belief relation, into acceptance of content as true, then, on this basis, as a reason for belief and action. Yet any adequate account of representation, illocution, locution and content _de se_ must enable, not collapse, this distinction.

Musholt constitutes a good example of commitment to the idea that at least in some cases one can speak of a self-ascriptive representational relation which plays this dual role. She gives an extended account[^14] of how the modes of perception and bodily experience so set their subject S in relation to a property or propositional function that thereby a form of representation about S is constituted of which one may truly assert such sentences as “S sees a tree then\* and there\*,” hence “S believes that a tree is in front of it\* then\* and there\*,” “S sees a large truck hurtling then\* and thence\* towards it\*,” etc. Moreover, in elaborating this account, Musholt mentions others as precedents for the idea, _inter alia_ Perry and, in particular, Recanati. The latter, she says, endorses a view “similar to the view defended here” since according to Recanati

>experiential states such as those involved in perception and bodily experience [proprioception and the like] are self-less in the sense that they do not represent the self. _Rather, the specific relation that the content of these states bears to the subject is provided by the experiential mode._ (Musholt 2015, p. 82; italics added)

Yet how could experiential mode possibly play this role—the role, that is, of ensuring that the subject[^15] of representation or illocution, in its capacity as the subject of representation or illocution, is constitutive of the identity of content, such that content _qua_ what is individuated by a ‘that’-clause requires either the first-person or the asterisked third-person pronoun? Implicitly, Pagin is asking this question. Indeed, implicitly he is also asking whether one can, on the property-theoretic or propositionally functional conception, still distinguish in any coherent fashion between mode and content at all.

Let us take the latter question first. Consider the following passage from Neil Feit:

>The theory of content that I shall be defending is not new. It was developed by Roderick Chisholm and David Lewis (independently) in the late 1970’s. According to this theory, the content of my belief that I am left-handed is not a proposition; it is a property. In particular, it is the property being left-handed. (Feit 2008, p. iii)[^16]

Here Feit unambiguously states that according to Chisholm and Lewis, indeed according to him\* himself, the content of a representation can sometimes be a property rather than a proposition. When, however, S believes that it\*, S, is F and S<sup>&#9670;</sup> believes that it\*, S<sup>&#9670;</sup>, is F, the same property or propositional function is involved yet content in the sense of what one would ordinarily describe as what is represented is different.[^17] So content as standardly understood, that is, when understood as what accounts for causal role and is individuated by a ‘that’-clause, cannot be simply identical with a property or propositional function. Feit should have made clear from the outset that in speaking thus, he is using the word ‘content’ non-standardly. In particular, he should have explained that he is using the word to connote what one is related to by the self-ascriptive mode B\*(_x_, _y is F_) or by whatever other self-ascriptive modes there might be, say, the perceptually self-ascriptive mode P\*(_x_, _y is F_), the conatively self-ascriptive mode D\*(_x_, _y is F_) or indeed the assertorically self-ascriptive mode A\*(_x_, _y is F_). This kind of contentfulness explains, he should have said, contentfulness in the aforementioned standard sense.

Appreciation that Feit is speaking too casually yields further insight: if one speaks as he does, then not only can one not mean by content what explains causal role, one also cannot speak of mode or force in the standard sense. Precisely for this reason, Loar writes, not ‘Believes(_x_, _y is F_)’, which is solecistic, but ‘B\*(\*_x_, _y is F_)’, which is neologistic: short of the definition by which it was introduced, one has no idea what ‘B\*(_x_, _y is F_)’ means.[^18] Nor will it do to fall back upon stipulation-*cum*-hypothesis-redeemed-by-theoretical-success. This only works if there is no undeniable feature of representation _de se_ rendered impossible by the property-theoretic or propositionally functional idea. Yet Pagin is arguing that there _is_ such a feature. In fact, his complaint is the following: _because_ “the connection between the content and the [subject] is not representational,” that is, _because_ “the [subject] is not represented … in the content of the thought,” there is nothing left but “the … mode, or … force, in the form of self-ascription” to make “the connection” and this will not do. (Pagin 2016, p. 275) For even if in some way one still can speak of “ … _mode_, or … _force_, in the form of self-ascription,” this must occur wherever self-ascription is. So the general idea itself leaves only one thing left for the subject to entertain _without_ standing in the relation B\*(_x_, _y is F_), namely, the property self-ascribed, or as Pagin perhaps more accurately puts it, “the representation of the property.” This is precisely why Feit speaks as he does—wrongly, as we now see.

It remains only to add that, _pace_ Pagin, “this does … matter for belief” and it matters precisely because belief has, as Pagin puts it, “by definition … force.” (Pagin 2016, p. 275) Indeed, it matters for belief in _exactly_ the same way in which it matters for communication. Even for a believing and not just a communicating subject, it must be possible not simply to “automatically believe” but to understand what is believed without _eo ipso_ believing it. Only then can a potential believer entertain a content in the sense of considering whether he\* or she\* has sufficient reason for believing it. So this content cannot be merely the property ascribed but rather content in the aforementioned standard sense, i.e., that which constitutes difference in causal role between beliefs, hence requires for its perspicuous individuation ‘that’-clauses involving different references even as the property ascribed is the same. Entertaining something in the sense implicated in the rational acquisition of belief requires that what is entertained be a content in this quotidian sense. And since a belief can only be acquired and possessed rationally if one is able to reflect on _one’s\*_ content, thereby coming to know what it is _one\*_ is taking as a candidate for _one’s\*_ rational belief, the attribution of representations and, for that matter, locutions and illocutions to oneself as _one\*’s own_ similarly requires that what is entertained be a content in the everyday sense.[^19] Only content in this sense is content a subject can relate to it without representing or illocuting it. And such relation to such content is required both for the rational acquisition of belief and for knowledge of belief as one\*’s own.

This point may be generalised to non-cognitive forms of representation since these, too, must be capable of both rational acquisition and self-attribution. For ultimately, the point Pagin seeks to make does not turn on the fact that belief or any other kind of representation or illocution “_has_, as he puts it, force.” (Pagin 2016, p. 275) Rather, it turns on _how_ representation or illocution “has force,” namely, in such a way that one maintains a clear distinction between ascription of a property F to something _a_ in the sense of believing or asserting that _a_ is F; and ascription of a property F to something _a_ in the sense of having as a content of belief or assertion something whose perspicuous expression requires predicating ‘… is F’, hence derivatively F-ness, of _a_. In other words, for rationality and self-attribution to be possible, one must insist on a distinction between self-ascription in a representational or illocutionary sense—as one might put it, self-ascription at the level of mode—; and self-ascription in a predicative sense—self-ascription at the level of content. _De facto_, the property-theoretic or propositionally functional conception obliterates this distinction.[^20]

But surely, one might object, the entertaining of a thought is itself a representational mode. So why not claim that what Loar and others have done for belief and desire can also be done for it? Why not suggest taking the notion of entertaining to be such that when Cynthia entertains the thought that she\* has the flu, what is true is E\*(Cynthia, _y has the flu_)? Clearly, even if the entertaining of a thought is in some sense a representational mode, this suggestion is simple-minded. When one speaks, say, in connection with communication, of entertaining a thought, i.e., a content, what is at issue is consideration of whether the content proffered to one for acceptance as true is true. Thus, when in this sense Cynthia entertains the content that she\* has the flu, she is considering whether she\* has sufficient reason for believing it. What, therefore, one needs to suggest is that when Cynthia entertains the content that she\* has the flu, what is true is E\*(Cynthia, _y has sufficient reason for believing that she\* has the flu_). This revised formulation of the self-ascriptive entertaining relation immediately shows the asterisked third-person pronoun to be indispensable. One cannot suggest, for example, that what is true is that E\*(Cynthia, *z has sufficient reason for B\*(Cynthia, y has the flu)*). For this would entail that Cynthia, in believing rationally and self-consciously that she\* has the flu, such that she has this belief under her rational control, hence could ascribe it to herself\*, also believed that she\* is Cynthia. Needless to say, this entailment is invalid and the only way to block it would be to deny that when Cynthia believes that she\* has the flu, what is true is B\*(Cynthia, _y has the flu_). Thereby, however, one would have undermined “the self-ascriptive belief relation” entirely. It immediately follows that Cynthia cannot know that the belief up for her rational consideration is belief that she\* has the flu if either the belief itself or the entertaining of its content is understood in terms of the property-theoretic or propositionally functional conception. The self-ascriptive entertaining relation E\*(_x_, _y has sufficient reason for believing that **p**_) only confirms what its introduction was meant to refute.

## Concluding Diagnosis

The general idea of a self-ascriptive representational or illocutionary relation is thus confused. It conflates self-ascription in a sense entailing some kind of representational or illocutionary force with self-ascription in a sense presupposed by the latter, namely, the _predicative_ ascription of a property to the subject of representation or illocution in this latter’s capacity as subject. Insofar as one means by such predicative ascription quite literally an act of predication, one is speaking of the combining of a predicate expressing a property or propositional function with either the first-person or the asterisked third-person pronoun. Insofar as one means the psychological or illocutionary reality perspicuously characterised by the whole ascriptive sentence in which this complex of predicate and pronoun is embedded, one is speaking of a content whose structure requires this linguistic complex, hence the appropriate pronoun, for its perspicuous characterisation, this because the complex reflects the very structure of the content characterised.

What, however, _explains_ why these two senses of the word ‘self-ascription’ must never be collapsed, not even in merely some cases, is that representation, illocution and its content must be entertainable, at least possibly if not actually, by its subject. For only so is content such that a subject might be able to reflect on the wisdom of undertaking what Searle calls commitment to it.[^21] If, however, self-ascription in the force-entailing sense must fundamentally differ from, even as it presupposes, self-ascription in the predicative sense, then the pronouns with which content _de se_ is characterised must be taken as referring _in virtue of an element in content_. Just this element explains why each pronoun, each in its different but interconnected way, refers. Yet neither brings any individual concept, hence Fregean sense with it, at least not as Loar conceives such sense. Nor can the element each expresses be first-personally indexical, _not even in cases of explicit self-representation_.

The _early_ Perry was therefore right: in order to solve what he calls the problem of the essential indexical, “we must” _not_ “… incorporate the indexical element into” (Perry 1979, p. 35/p. 12) content. And as already pointed out, the property-theoretic or propositionally functional conception is in large part motivated by suspicion of _first-personally indexical_ elements in content. So let me end by justifying the suspicion. Talk of first-person or self-*concepts* is expression of embarrassment: by definition a concept can apply to several different things yet by definition a self-concept cannot. Nor are self-*notions* less embarrassing. No doubt one can have notion of something in a sense entailing that the perspicuous expression thereof refers in definitely definitional fashion to a single individual, as when one has a notion of who killed JFK. But a notion in this sense does not apply rigidly to something, unlike a self-notion and its cousin, the self-concept. In any case, notions in this sense, precisely because they are expressed by definite descriptions, do not address the problem which introduction of first-personally indexical elements in content was meant to solve. Nor does it help to talk, with Castañeda, of ‘I’-guises or with Frege, of ways in which a subject S is given to itself, presumably as itself\*. Guises and ways of givenness are not only more obscure than concepts or notions, _prima facie_ they can also apply to multiple individuals. Smith can wear the same guise as Jones and be given in the same way as Jones.

Implicit here is an important result for which I have not sufficiently argued. To use Perry’s jargon,[^22] the sentence “I am making a mess” no less ‘identifies’ a proposition than does the sentence “Perry is making a mess.” As asserted by Perry, the former ‘identifies’ the proposition that he\*, John Perry, is making a mess, just as the latter ‘identifies’ the different proposition that John Perry is making a mess. A careful reading of what Perry calls “the traditional doctrine of propositions” (Perry 1979, p. 32/p. 8) shows that, _pace_ Perry, it does _not_ rule the former out; only a covert prohibition on such only anaphorically expressible contents does this. It is, however, merely to make this same point in another way to claim that an element in content corresponds equally to the first-person and asterisked third-person pronouns—one and the same element which only the latter articulates perspicuously precisely because it is not first-personally indexical, hence permits capture of what content is for all subjects, no matter who, when or where they are.

## Appendix—Response to Two Objections

When S seriously and literally asserts, “I am F,” what S asserts is that it\* is F, _end of story_. There is no need to construe either S’s use of the first-person pronoun or my use of the asterisked third-person pronoun as corresponding to or marking anything first-personally indexical in the content of S’s assertion. Nor is there, given sincerity on S’s part, anything first-personally indexical in the content of S’s belief. When S either asserts or believes, “I am F,” equivalently, when S either asserts or believes that it\* is F, any claim that the pronoun marks, indeed cannot but mark, a distinctively _first-personal_ or _indexical_ element in content is just plain false. Both pronouns mark the same element in content while the asterisked third-person pronoun captures this element as it is for all subjects. In this sense, it captures the element as it objectively is, hence in this entirely stipulative sense, as it ‘really’ is.

Against this claim one might make two important objections both of which argue in different ways that by excluding first-personal indexicality from content, one fails to reconstruct certain crucial features of S’s assertion and belief. Firstly, one might argue that if it were true, the claim would render self-ascription, whether of representations or locutions, inexplicable, even impossible. Secondly, one might argue that the claim fails to capture something essential to the content of both S’s assertion and belief, what, namely, one might call, in a manner deriving from Cappelen and Dever,[^23] its personalness. Let us take the first objection first.

When a self-conscious subject S ascribes something in either its _Bewusstseins-_ or indeed its _Sprachstrom_ to itself, or rather, to itself\*, it is not as if S paradigmatically confronted a stream of representations or locutions of whose subject it were unaware, as if these representations or locutions were given to it as ownerless, as if S still had to ask, “But whose are these?” From the outset, S is paradigmatically aware of either its representations or its locutions as its\*. So in any explicitly linguistic act of self-ascription, S wields _from the outset_ the first-person pronoun in subject position: “I assert that _p_,” “I believe that _p_,” “I order that _p_,” “I desire that _p_,” indeed, “I see an F G-ing,” etc. Let us now assume that S asserts or believes that a large pit bull is charging aggressively towards it\*, sees a large pit bull charging aggressively towards it\*, and presumably that S desires that a large pit bull not be charging aggressively towards it\*. Clearly, given that S is paradigmatically aware from the outset of all these locutionary and representational items as its\*, it immediately follows, given the sense the asterisked third-person pronoun has on the occasion, that in using the first-person pronoun in the subject-position of the relevant intensional context, S also _eo ipso_ uses the first-person pronoun in that place of the third-personal clause expressing content which is occupied by the asterisked third-person pronoun. In no way, however, does the possibility of this self-ascription _require_ that the asterisked third-person pronoun mark anything itself first-personally indexical.

Let us turn now to the second objection. Given how the (S)-use has been characterised in the Introduction, it is just not true that clauses involving the asterisked third-person pronoun fail to capture the personalness of content, hence representation and locution _de se_. A personal content is one which only its representer or locuter can possess as a content of representation or locution, as opposed to such impersonal contents as that John Perry is making a mess on 07.07.79 in the supermarket. And this is all that is required in order to explain why Perry acted as he did is, not any alleged indexicality of content itself, as opposed to the indexicality of how Perry would express this his\* content. That only such personalness, which is articulated perfectly adequately by clauses containing the asterisked third-person pronoun, is required is shown by the fact that Perry could have written his famous paper entirely in the third-person pronoun rather than indulging in his ultimately misleading first-personal presentation.[^24]

Note, moreover, that this point dissipates all worries about “propositions of limited accessibility.” (Perry 1979, p. 37/p. 15) Yes, only one subject, namely, Perry himself, can assert or believe that he\*, John Perry, is making a mess. In fact, given that he is asserting and believing on 07.07.79 in the supermarket, Perry can only assert or believe that he\* is making a mess then\* and there\* on 07.07.79 in the supermarket. Evans asks rhetorically, “What is so absurd about the idea that there are thoughts,” i.e., contents, “which one can _have_ only because one occupies a particular position in space, or time … ?” (Evans 1981, p. 314; italics added) Nothing at all! And so there is nothing absurd about _personal_ contents, i.e., ones one can only _have_ as a content of representation or locution if one is a particular subject. But this _ontological_ privacy does not render them _epistemically_ private, at least not if one acknowledges that what they ‘really’ are is captured entirely by third-person anaphoric characterisation using the asterisked third-person pronoun. What does Perry assert when he seriously and literally asserts the sentence, “I am making a mess”? That he\* is making a mess! Anyone can ‘grasp’ this ‘thought’ in the sense of _knowing what content of representation or locution it is_ even though they cannot ‘grasp’ it in the sense of _having it as a content of representation or locution_. In fact, such ontologically private contents only become epistemically inaccessible to others if and because one construes the element marked by either the first-person or the asterisked third-person pronoun as _itself_ first-personally indexical. But there is no need for such construal.

Admittedly, one might dispute the very suggestion that contents expressed by the asterisked third-person pronoun, such that thereby the contribution of content to causal role is captured, are ontologically private. In this case, in order to preserve what is distinctive about first-personal locutions, one would be thrown back to the idea of a distinctively epistemic privacy engendered by the presence of a first-personally indexical element in content. As an argument to this effect, one might offer the following account of how S<sup>&#9670;</sup> comes to believe what S intends this latter to believe when S asserts, in communicative intent, the sentence “I am making a mess”:

>What S said was that it\* is making a mess and so S<sup>&#9670;</sup>, because this latter trusts S, formed the belief that it\*, _S_, is making a mess.

Surely this is perfectly possible, in which case contents *de se* are *not* ontologically private. So something has been lost in the shift from the so-called first-person to the third-person perspective and in order to recover it, one must regard the content of S’s assertion as first-personal in a way which prevents it from being at least _fully_ accessible to anyone but S itself.

The argument is, however, flawed. If one examines closely the characterisation of the (S)-use given in the Introduction, one sees relatively quickly that it is in fact incorrect to use the *asterisked* third-person pronoun in order to characterise the content _of the belief formed by S<sup>&#9670;</sup>_. In other words, use of the third-person pronoun ‘it’ in the characterisation of this content is not and cannot be an (S)-use. When the third-person pronoun is (S)-used, the user of it refers to something as subject of a representation or locution _whose content the user is using the pronoun, more precisely put, a clause containing the pronoun, to characterise_. Here, however, in the second 'that'-clause of the account offered, the content being characterised is that of S<sup>&#9670;</sup>’s belief, not that _of S’s locution_. This is not to deny, of course, that use of the third-person pronoun in the second clause, a clear example of an anaphoric tie to the singular term ‘S’, is perfectly possible and legitimate; it is merely to deny that this is an (S)-use. In fact, its use constitutes _imperspicuous_ characterisation of content since the belief S<sup>&#9670;</sup> actually forms, and is intended by S to form, is that S, or the person it\*, S<sup>&#9670;</sup>, is conversing with, etc., is making a mess. The use made here of the third-person pronoun is thus analogous to that made _by \*S<sup>&#9670;</sup>_ of the _first_-person pronoun  in the following account:

>What S said was that I, _S<sup>&#9670;</sup>_, am making a mess and so I, _S<sup>&#9670;</sup>_, because I trust S, formed the belief that I am making a mess.

This, too, is perfectly possible and legitimate but it clearly does not involve _perspicuous_ characterisation of what S said.

So what _did_ S say in this latter case? Presumably, although not, of course, necessarily, in this latter case S has asserted the sentence, “You are making a mess.” But what exactly has S said in asserting this sentence? Note that it would be at best phenomenologically implausible to maintain that S is asserting, hence saying, at _t_ and _l_ that the person it\* is addressing then\* and there\* is making a mess then\* and there\* in order to get this latter to believe what it\* is asserting then\* and there\*. This implausibility creates an opening for an extremely interesting extension of the idea that Castañeda’s asterisk, more precisely put, the (S)-use which it marks, constitutes, as Castañeda himself put it, “a _unique logical_ category, which is not analyzable in terms of any other type of referring mechanism (i.e., the other uses of ‘he’, other personal pronouns, proper names, demonstratives, and definite descriptions).” (Castañeda 1966, p. 35) For it gestures towards the following hypothesis: S is seeking at _t_ and _l_ to get S<sup>&#9670;</sup> to believe that _it<sup>&#9670;</sup>_, _S<sup>&#9670;</sup>_, is making a mess then\* and there\*, this by asserting then\* and there\* that _it<sup>&#9670;</sup>_, _S<sup>&#9670;</sup>_, is making a mess then\* and there\*. Here, the anaphoric link is not to S in its capacity as _subject_ of locution and illocution, but to S<sup>&#9670;</sup> in its capacity as an _object_ thereof, i.e., as a target of perlocution-*via*-illocution.
Evidently, if this is right, then there is a sense in which one may speak, not just of a first- and third-person perspective, _but also of an ineliminable second-person one_. Not only the potential ineliminability of locutions and contents _de te_, but also the peculiar structure given to them by this hypothesis, makes such contents extremely interesting. For if it is right, then the dimensions of locution and illocution are conceptually intertwined with the dimension of genuinely communicative perlocution even though locution and illocution can, at least as exceptions to the rule, occur in the absence of perlocutionary intent. Moreover, truth of the hypothesis would create conceptual room, indeed would cry out for the idea that the second person pronoun is a means for referring to another not just as someone upon whom its user seeks to have such and such an effect,[^25] but as a partner in communication. Thereby it insinuates a claim both plausible and appealing, namely, that locution and illocution are founded, as a condition of their possibility, in acts of communication in which another appears not simply as someone to be affected but as someone _recognised_ (_anerkannt_) from the outset as a partner in communication. With this, one arrives at a notion of perlocution which saves it from the simplistic idea manifest in, e.g., Griceanism that communication is just a matter of having effects on others.
